Numbers don’t land.
Places do.

Almost everyone knows the camps existed. Very few have any real sense of how big they were. Size of Pain takes the real, surveyed footprint of a Nazi camp and lays it, at true physical size, over a place you already know: your city, your neighbourhood, the street you grew up on.

Or open the map at the real site and drag it anywhere.

How it works

  1. 1

    Choose a camp

    Each camp is drawn from its documented perimeter, barracks, ruins, railways and watchtowers as mapped on OpenStreetMap.

  2. 2

    Drop it somewhere you know

    Search a city, use your location, or drag the outline. It is redrawn at its true size wherever it lands, never stretched by the map projection.

  3. 3

    Explore the layers

    Switch structures on and off and open i Learn more on each layer to understand what it was and what happened there.

The camps

Every outline below is drawn to the same scale, so you can compare them directly. Choose one to open it on the map.

Outlines follow what is mapped today. Where the original perimeter does not survive, the outline is the memorial grounds, which may be smaller than the camp was. Each camp's page says which.

What the layers show

Every camp is split into the same kinds of structures. On the map, each layer has its own Learn more with what it meant at that particular camp.

Why this exists

“1.69 km²” or “the size of 236 football pitches” is a fact you can read and immediately forget. It does not land, because it has nothing to compare itself to inside your own head. This project tries to close that gap: not a lack of awareness that it happened, but a lack of tools to make the scale of it felt rather than just stated.

Scale cannot describe the human loss. This map is an educational spatial comparison and cannot represent the lives, suffering, and histories of the people persecuted and murdered by Nazi Germany. For the history of each site, please visit the memorials linked on every camp.

Data and method

Geometry © OpenStreetMap contributors, ODbL. Each shape is stored as metres from the camp's centre and re-projected around wherever you place it, the same idea thetruesize.com uses for countries. Victim figures come from the memorial or museum responsible for each site. The code is open source on GitHub.

Developed by the Valencia Hub in collaboration with the Kraków Hub, both part of the World Economic Forum's Global Shapers Community.
